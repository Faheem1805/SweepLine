Problem statement
How might we advance the sustainable management of seasonal jellyfish blooms in coastal environments?

Early warning already exists. The challenge starts after it: manage large, continuous swarms with less manual effort, no harm to marine life and no redesign of plant systems.

Proposed solution

Two full-depth nets stand across the intake channel at 20°, ahead of every intake. Water passes through. Jellyfish cannot, so the plant’s own flow sweeps them along the net to a collector. A slow screw lift sends them over the breakwater and 2 km down the coast. No boats. No crews. No change to the plant.

Technical explanation

2 million m³/h through a 140 × 12 m channel runs at 0.33 m/s. A 20° net splits that: 0.11 m/s through the mesh, inside the 0.12 m/s fish-screen limit, and 0.31 m/s along it, so animals slide instead of pinning. For any channel, net length = flow ÷ (depth × 0.12 m/s). At 0.12 m of head the float line dips, so it can never act as a dam.

Solution features: what goes wrong, and what we did

The tide turnsThe plant sets the flow. It never reverses.

They swim underNets reach from surface to seabed.

A panel tearsA second net 60 m behind catches them.

The net clogsThe float line dips. Cooling comes first.

Power failsNets need none. They hold the bloom.

A storm hitsThe breakwaters shelter the nets.

Night, murky waterSonar and load cells need no light.

Gulf foulingPanels swapped every 4 weeks, out off-season.

Pumps shred animalsA screw lift has no impeller. No air either.

A new species50 mm mesh stops every bell size modelled.
